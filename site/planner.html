Pre-Test Planner

Answer each planning question before preparing articles.

The planner turns your study needs and physical limits into a preparation estimate. It does not make a final reliability claim.

Read the estimate in groups

The main study estimate is the first group to prepare. Reserve group 1 and reserve group 2 stay separate. They are not used automatically: at each checkpoint, you decide whether the next reserve group is needed.

The planner uses a 400 independent articles safety floor before it can issue a supported reliability setting. That is a conservative rule supported by this project’s recorded simulations, not a universal Neyer number and not an instruction to run 400 articles in every study.

Separate optional fixed-condition demonstration: v1.10 does not include the optional fixed-gap zero-failure qualification planner. That optional demonstration checks one already-chosen gap, rather than estimating the Neyer response curve. 298 is the specific fixed-condition rule; 299 is the conservative general calculation; neither is a Neyer curve-study quantity.

Planning question

What it means: Choose whether to start with the requirements you need or with the number of articles you already have.

Example: Choose requirements first when you need 99% reliability at 95% confidence. Choose articles available first when only 120 separate articles can be prepared.

Important: When articles are fixed, keep either reliability or confidence fixed so the planner can estimate the other one. It never invents both.

Maximum articles available

What it means: The number of separate destructive articles that can be prepared for this study.

Example: Enter 120 when the laboratory can prepare 120 articles in total.

Important: This is not a count of repeat readings. A destructive article is tested once and cannot become a later test article.

Keep this requirement fixed

What it means: In articles-available-first planning, choose whether reliability or confidence stays fixed while the planner estimates the other value.

Example: Keep reliability at 99% when you need to learn the confidence supported by the articles available.

Important: This choice appears only when articles available is the starting point. It prevents the planner from claiming both targets without enough evidence.

Required physical result

What it means: Select the result the final operating instruction should aim to produce: Interaction or No interaction.

Example: Choose Interaction when contact is required at the operating gap; choose No interaction when separation is required.

Important: Smaller physical gaps make Interaction more likely. Larger physical gaps make No interaction more likely.

Reliability (%)

What it means: Reliability is the minimum fraction of similar articles expected to give the required physical result.

Example: 99% reliability asks for an operating instruction that is expected to give the chosen result for 99 out of 100 similar articles.

Important: Reliability is not confidence and does not promise the result for one individual article.

Confidence (%)

What it means: Confidence describes how strongly the completed evidence must support the reliability answer.

Example: 95% confidence asks for a stronger evidence margin than 90% confidence.

Important: Confidence is not the percentage of articles expected to interact. Reliability answers that different question.

Required gap accuracy (+/- mm)

What it means: This is how close the middle-gap estimate needs to be, in millimetres. The middle gap is the approximately 50/50 response point.

Example: A requested middle-gap accuracy of +/-0.10 mm means the study needs a middle estimate close enough for a tenth-of-a-millimetre decision.

Important: This accuracy check is not the separate operating instruction. Ask only for accuracy that the confirmed physical builds can achieve; a calculation cannot make an unreachable gap buildable.

Almost-always Interaction gap (mm)

What it means: Enter a smaller gap where Interaction is expected almost every time.

Example: Enter 1.00 mm when similar articles are expected to interact at that small gap.

Important: “Almost always” is not certainty. For a first study, the planner treats this endpoint as at least 95%, not 100%.

Almost-always No-interaction gap (mm)

What it means: Enter a larger gap where No interaction is expected almost every time.

Example: Enter 10.00 mm when similar articles are expected not to interact at that larger gap.

Important: This is the opposite endpoint from the Interaction gap, not a promise about every individual article.

Earlier information

What it means: Tell the planner whether this is a first study or whether earlier results changed suddenly or gradually.

Example: Choose “Results change gradually” when earlier work suggests the change happens across a broad range of gaps.

Important: Earlier information is an assumption for planning, not a replacement for the new study’s measured results.

Minimum permitted gap (mm)

What it means: Set the smallest physical gap the equipment and study rules permit.

Example: Enter 0.00 mm when no-spacer contact is permitted.

Important: Do not lower this boundary merely because an estimate falls below it. The permitted range comes from the physical setup and safety rules.

Maximum permitted gap (mm)

What it means: Set the largest useful physical gap for this setup.

Example: Enter 10.00 mm when that is the largest planned gap.

Important: A larger gap that is already obviously No interaction adds no useful evidence, and an estimate outside the permitted range is not a build instruction.

Physical setup method

What it means: Choose regular increments, spacer and foil combinations, or a confirmed reachable-gap list to describe what can actually be built.

Example: Choose spacer and foil combinations when printed spacers make whole millimetres and foil adds smaller measured components.

Important: Use only confirmed reachable gaps. Approximately 0.015 mm foil thickness is construction information, not proof of a 0.015 mm usable test step.

Regular increment (mm)

What it means: Set the repeating physical increment when every requested multiple can genuinely be built.

Example: Enter 0.05 mm when the setup can repeatedly build 2.45 mm, 2.50 mm, and later 0.05 mm changes.

Important: Choose this method only for genuine regular builds. It must not be replaced by foil thickness.

Confirmed gaps (mm)

What it means: List only individual buildable gaps already checked by measurement.

Example: A confirmed list might be 0, 0.5, 1, 2, and 10 mm.

Important: Nominal spacer labels are not measurements. Include only gaps the laboratory can actually repeat.

Measured components and maximum count

What it means: Enter each spacer or foil component’s measured size and the greatest number of layers the setup permits.

Example: Enter a measured foil layer and the maximum number of foil layers that can be used with a printed spacer.

Important: The planner combines measured components, not nominal labels. Measure the actual build before using it in a destructive test.

Usable gap step

What it means: The usable gap step is the smallest physical change this study can reliably request and build during the test workflow.

Example: A 0.05 mm usable gap step can support requested build gaps such as 2.45 mm and 2.50 mm.

Important: Approximately 0.015 mm foil thickness is construction information, not proof of a 0.015 mm usable test step.
